## 1. Naphtali-Sandholm (simultaneous correction) vs inside-out vs bubble-point / sum-rates: cost per iteration, property evaluations, robustness

### Takeaway
Tearing methods (BP, SR) and the classic case for inside-out are all about *where the expensive property calls sit*:
BP/SR evaluate rigorous properties several times per outer loop; inside-out moves rigorous calls to an outer loop and
iterates cheap local models (ln K = a + b/T, linear enthalpy) inside; Newton/Naphtali-Sandholm evaluates rigorous
properties *and their derivatives* once per iteration, converges quadratically in about 30 iterations or fewer, and is
more robust for non-ideal mixtures, but its per-iteration cost is dominated by the Jacobian of K-values and
enthalpies, so how that Jacobian is built (analytic vs finite-difference, sparse vs dense) decides whether it is fast.
No open source found gives a head-to-head CPU-time number between inside-out and Naphtali-Sandholm.

### Cited Findings
- Physical-property calculations "generally dominate the computational cost of chemical process simulation problems" (citing Westerberg et al. 1979; Baden and Michelsen 1987); the first stated problem with tearing methods is "the number of times physical properties must be evaluated (several times per outer loop iteration)" when T- and composition-dependent properties are used — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4, p. 262-263](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- BP method (Wang and Henke 1966, Thomas algorithm) and SR method (Sujata 1961; Friday 1963; Burningham and Otto 1967) are summarized as nested loops: K-values re-evaluated in the inner loop, tridiagonal solve per component, then T from bubble-point (BP) or from enthalpy balances (SR); BP "supposedly works 'well'" for narrow-boiling, SR for wide-boiling (Friday and Smith 1964); Sridhar and Lucia (1990) gave modified BP/SR methods with analytical Newton acceleration of the inner loop for narrow, intermediate and wide-boiling mixtures — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.3.3-13.3.4, 13.3.8, Tables 13.3-13.4, p. 257-262](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Inside-out (Boston and Sullivan 1974; Boston and Britt 1978; Boston 1980): "complicated equilibrium and enthalpy expressions were replaced by simple models and the iteration variables T, V, x and y were replaced by variables within the models which are relatively free of interactions with each other"; drawback: "quite problem specific requiring new software for each type of specification, flash problem, etc."; "IO methods have replaced many of the older algorithms in commercial simulation programs, the modified version of Russell (1983) being the basis for some of them" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.3.6, p. 258](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf) (secondary for the original IO papers, which were not opened)
- An open-source implementation (NeqSim 3.9.1, DistillationColumn) documents its inside-out inner loop as ln K_i = a_i + b_i/T per component per tray, fitted from rigorous flash results at the current and previous outer-loop temperatures; "Between outer-loop updates, compositions are estimated using this simplified model instead of full PH-flash calculations, reducing computational cost by a factor of approximately innerLoopSteps" (developer's own claim, not a benchmark) — [NeqSim API doc, DistillationColumn.SimplifiedKvalueModel](https://equinor.github.io/neqsimhome/javadoc/site/apidocs/neqsim/process/equipment/distillation/DistillationColumn.SimplifiedKvalueModel.html)
- Simultaneous correction: "generally fast and reliable, having a locally quadratic convergence rate", "much less sensitive to difficulties associated with nonideal solutions than are tearing methods"; extends to interlinked columns, non-standard specifications and stage efficiencies with "only minor changes" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4, p. 264](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Formulations: 2c+3 (L, V, T, y, x), 2c+1 (component flows l, v and T; Naphtali-Sandholm), c+3 (Goldstein and Stanfield 1970). In 2c+1 "the material balance equations are linear and will, if Newton's method is used, be satisfied on every iteration after the first"; c+3 is cheaper per linear solve but cannot account for vapour-composition dependence of K. "The 2c+1 formulation is probably the best choice for single columns" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.2, p. 264-265](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Grouping by stage (Naphtali-Sandholm) gives a block-tridiagonal Jacobian, preferred when stages outnumber components; grouping by type preferred when components outnumber stages — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.3-13.4.4, p. 265-266](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Linear solve: the Jacobian has s²(2c+3)² elements, "straightforward matrix inversion is totally impractical"; block-tridiagonal Thomas algorithm, further improved because A and C blocks "are nearly empty" (Baden 1984; Baden and Michelsen 1987); ChemSep itself now uses a sparse Gaussian elimination solver (NSPIV) because pumparounds and non-standard specs break the block structure — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.5, p. 268](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Iteration counts: "Typically, we find that 30 Newton iterations suffices to solve most separation problems"; more iterations usually signal infeasible specs or oscillation — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.6, p. 269](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Robustness: "An unconstrained Newton's method should converge at least 75% of all separation process problems with no particular difficulty. Adding bells and whistles in the form of damping factors, line searches and the like will increase the probability of success to around 95%" (authors' experience, no test set given) — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.5, p. 271-272](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- ChemSep's default damping: max 10 K temperature change per iteration, max 0.5 fractional flow change, a mole fraction about to go negative or above one moves half the distance to the bound; "Newton method loses its effectiveness when damped" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.8-13.4.9, p. 271](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- DWSIM (open source) offers Wang-Henke bubble point (default `SolvingMethodName = "Wang-Henke (Bubble Point)"`), a modified BP, Burningham-Otto sum rates, and Naphtali-Sandholm (`NewtonRaphson.vb`), plus a Tomich solver class; no inside-out column solver (it has Boston-Britt inside-out and Boston-Fournier three-phase inside-out *flash* algorithms only) — [DWSIM RigorousColumn.vb, lines ~1914 and 4807-4861](https://github.com/DanWBR/dwsim/blob/windows/DWSIM.UnitOperations/UnitOperations/RigorousColumn.vb); [DWSIM FlashAlgorithms folder](https://github.com/DanWBR/dwsim/tree/windows/DWSIM.Thermodynamics/FlashAlgorithms)
- DWSIM's Naphtali-Sandholm builds a *dense* finite-difference Jacobian: `FunctionGradient(0.001, x)` perturbs every variable down and up (relative step 0.001) and calls the full residual function twice per variable, i.e. 2n full-column residual evaluations per Jacobian, each recomputing K-values and enthalpies on all stages (stages run in `Parallel.For`). It first tries MathNet Broyden root finding, then its own Newton solver with Broyden approximation and damping (`MaximumDelta = 0.2`, `ExpandFactor = 1.6`), then full Newton — [DWSIM NewtonRaphson.vb, lines 232-345, 669-705, 1060-1125](https://github.com/DanWBR/dwsim/blob/windows/DWSIM.UnitOperations/UnitOperations/RigorousColumnSolvers/NewtonRaphson.vb)

### Inferences
- For an n-stage, c-component Naphtali-Sandholm column, a finite-difference Jacobian built column-wide (DWSIM style) costs about 2·N(2c+1) full-column property sweeps per iteration, i.e. O(N²c) stage property calls; a stage-local finite-difference or analytic Jacobian costs O(Nc) (each stage's K and H depend only on that stage's T, x, y). Exploiting this block locality is probably the largest single speed-up available to a Newton column code that perturbs globally.
- Inside-out's advantage is that the number of rigorous property calls scales with outer-loop iterations (typically few), not with the inner iterations; the same idea can be grafted onto Newton as a "cheap-model inner Newton" or as a Jacobian built from local models (see section 3).

### Gaps
- No open primary source found with a measured CPU-time or property-call comparison of inside-out vs Naphtali-Sandholm on the same problems; the Boston-Sullivan 1974, Boston 1980 and Russell 1983 papers and Seader and Henley's chapter were not opened.
- Aspen RadFrac/HYSYS algorithm documentation (STANDARD, NONIDEAL, NEWTON, SUM-RATES options and why inside-out is the default) could not be retrieved in this session (the hosted copies of the Aspen Plus Input Language Guide and Physical Property Methods reference returned HTML, not PDFs). The statement that inside-out is the RadFrac/HYSYS default is therefore unverified here.

## 2. Three-phase (VLLE) columns: which stages get a second-liquid test, how often, and what it costs

### Takeaway
The open literature offers three strategies: (a) fix the phase count per stage in advance (Ferraris and Morbidelli),
judged unreliable; (b) a "mixed K-value / pseudo-single-liquid" model (Schuil and Bool 1985; Baden and Michelsen 1987)
that keeps the equation count fixed and folds the liquid split into averaged activity coefficients and enthalpy, so
the column solver never changes size; (c) a full stability test plus phase split on each stage each iteration
(Cairns and Furzer 1990; Skiborowski et al. 2015 per iteration of an optimization). A cheaper alternative, reported
as reliable on two lab systems, is to carry the VLLE equations and switch a stage to VLE only when a liquid flow
would have to go negative (Ottenbacher and Hasse 2006), avoiding tangent-plane tests. Most stages in a "three-phase"
column have one liquid, which is the reason testing every stage every iteration is wasteful.

### Cited Findings
- Why VLLE columns are hard: very high nonlinearity of K and H; "the number of equations and variables ... is not known until the problem has been solved", "there will almost certainly be a number (probably a majority) of stages with only one liquid phase present"; and the model "always admits the ('trivial') two-phase solution" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.7.1, p. 281-282](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Algorithms listed: Newton/simultaneous (Ferraris and Morbidelli 1981, 1982; Baden 1984; Baden and Michelsen 1987; Cairns and Furzer 1990), multistage flash, bubble-point type (Block and Hegner 1976; Kinoshita et al. 1983), inside-out with property evaluations in the outer loop (Boston and Shah 1979; Ross and Seider 1980; Prokopakis et al. 1981; Schuil and Bool 1985), homotopy (Kovach and Seider 1986a,b; Woodman et al. 1989) — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.7.1, p. 282](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- In the simultaneous-correction method of Ferraris and Morbidelli "it is necessary to specify in advance which stages have two phases and which stages have three phases. This is an unsatisfactory feature ... that makes convergence a rather uncertain adventure" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.7.1, p. 282](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Mixed K-value model (Schuil and Bool 1985, extending Niedzwiecki et al. 1980; similar in the SC method of Baden and Michelsen 1987): treat both liquids as one pseudo-liquid of overall composition; with α the fraction of liquid in phase ′, K_i = K′_i K″_i / (αK′_i + (1−α)K″_i), which reduces to a mixed activity coefficient γ_i = γ′_i γ″_i / (αγ′_i + (1−α)γ″_i), and mixed liquid enthalpy from H′ and H″; on one-liquid stages the actual liquid properties are used. "In either situation, the number of equations used to model a single stage is always known", and "existing two phase separation process computer programs" can be used — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.7.2, eq. 13.45-13.52, p. 283-284](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Cairns and Furzer (1990), Ind. Eng. Chem. Res. 29, Part 2 "Phase stability and phase splitting algorithms": an algorithm based on Michelsen's stability test and a phase-split step within the column calculation; predicted three-phase column performance agreed with their Part 1 experiments (abstract only) — [search summary of Cairns and Furzer 1990, DOI 10.1021/ie00103a041](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/991244493); citation also in [ChemSep Book references, p. ~290](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Kosuge and Mortaheb (rate-based, stage-by-stage heterogeneous azeotropic column, ethanol-benzene-water): "In order to reduce the calculation time, the effect of liquid phase resistance is only considered in the calculations in the heterogeneous liquid region", detected with "the modified tangent plane phase stability test proposed by Cairns and Furzer"; reported cost "about 10 seconds per tray for an accuracy of 1×10-4 with a personal computer of 800 MHz CPU" (rate-based model, not equilibrium; old hardware) — [Kosuge and Mortaheb, Distillation 2002, p. 6-7](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/prost/proceedings/distillation02/dokument/32-3.pdf)
- Ottenbacher and Hasse (continuous three-phase distillation, ethyl acetate-1-butanol-water and MIBK-butyl acetate-water, gPROMS): commercial simulators "including Aspen Plus" and direct Newton and Levenberg-Marquardt solutions of the MESH equations failed; the variable phase count plus three recycles "requires very accurate initial guesses". Phase-count handling: "As soon as one of the liquid streams leaving the equilibrium stage has to be negative to satisfy the MESH equations, this stream is set to zero and the system of equations is switched to the VLE case ... Both the (now hypothetical) VLLE and the VLE are henceforth calculated simultaneously. The same criterion is then used to detect a transition ... in the opposite direction. This method has worked very reliable and stable while requiring much less calculation effort than a fully-fledged phase stability test would, e.g. by tangent plane criterion." Comparable approach by Eckert and Kubicek — [Ottenbacher and Hasse 2006, p. 843-844](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/prost/proceedings/distillation06/CD-proceedings/paper082.pdf)
- Skiborowski, Harwardt and Marquardt (2015, Comput. Chem. Eng.): determine phase stability and correct equilibrium solutions "in every iteration of the optimization" with a homotopy continuation algorithm, and reformulate equilibrium and enthalpy calculations to avoid discontinuities (abstract only) — [TUHH Open Research record](https://tore.tuhh.de/entities/publication/2e55eb90-e9f1-4d8c-91e9-26560556df56)
- Liu, Mori, Hiraoka and Yamada (1993, J. Chem. Eng. Japan 26(1) 41-47): a stage-by-stage three-phase distillation algorithm "with the characteristics of rapid and reliable determination of the phase number on each stage" (abstract only; the J-STAGE PDF did not download) — [J-STAGE record](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jcej/26/1/26_1_41/_article/-char/ja)
- Bossen et al. (1993, Ind. Eng. Chem. Res. 32(4) 620-633, DTU): "a fast and efficient method for test of phase stability in simulation of distillation operations" is named as a key feature (abstract only, method not seen) — [OSTI record 6535000](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/6535000)
- DWSIM's rigorous column (all solvers read) computes stage K-values from liquid and vapour fugacity coefficients at the stage's current x and y (`DW_CalcKvalue(x, y, T, P)`); no per-stage second-liquid stability test appears in the Newton solver. A property package may set `ShouldUseKvalueMethod2` (default `False`), in which case K comes from a full PT flash of the stage's overall composition (`DW_CalcKvalue(z, T, P)` → `FlashBase.Flash_PT`), i.e. a flash, possibly with the flash algorithm's own stability analysis, on every stage at every residual evaluation, and inside the finite-difference Jacobian as well — [DWSIM NewtonRaphson.vb, lines 232-300](https://github.com/DanWBR/dwsim/blob/windows/DWSIM.UnitOperations/UnitOperations/RigorousColumnSolvers/NewtonRaphson.vb); [DWSIM PropertyPackage.vb, lines 524-526, 1607-1640](https://github.com/DanWBR/dwsim/blob/windows/DWSIM.Thermodynamics/PropertyPackages/PropertyPackage.vb)
- ChemSep added "a three phase distillation model" among its extensions (preface); the user chapter 9 "Three Phase Distillation" in the 2nd edition PDF is an empty placeholder, so ChemSep's own stage-selection rule is not documented there — [ChemSep Book, preface and ch. 9, p. 175](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Cost of stability analysis in flash-heavy simulators (reservoir simulation, transferable in kind): "the majority of time is spent doing stability analysis" (patent statement); Rasmussen, Krejbjerg, Michelsen and Bjurstrøm (SPE 84181, SPE Reserv. Eval. Eng. 2006) skip stability tests where the state changed little since the previous step, with a distance-to-critical safeguard; the DTU "shadow region method" uses previous results to skip stability analysis in single-phase regions (secondary: patent summaries and a DTU record, the SPE paper not opened) — [US 7,548,840 (Google Patents PDF)](https://patentimages.storage.googleapis.com/9e/b7/21/362a2e2b0b2b6a/US7548840.pdf); [DTU Orbit record](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/speeding-up-compositional-reservoir-simulation-through-an-efficie/)
- Ross and Seider found that the Murphree efficiency affects where, and whether, two liquids appear, so the location of three-phase trays "cannot be done reliably" without efficiency data — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.7.3, p. 286](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)

### Inferences
- Testing every stage every iteration is the most expensive option and is not what the reviewed methods recommend. Cheaper, published alternatives: (i) the mixed-K pseudo-liquid formulation, which keeps the Newton system a fixed size and needs an LLE split only on stages already known to be split; (ii) carrying both VLLE and VLE on candidate stages and switching on a negative-flow ("negative flash") criterion; (iii) a full tangent-plane test only when a stage's (T, x) moved more than a threshold since its last test (shadow-region idea), or only at start and near convergence as a final check.
- Since the trivial one-liquid solution always satisfies the MESH equations, a scheme that skips tests must still do a final stability check of every stage at convergence, or it can silently return the trivial solution (this follows from the ChemSep Book's point 3; it is not a reported technique).

### Gaps
- Aspen RadFrac's own rules ("Valid phases: Vapor-Liquid-Liquid", "stages to test for two liquid phases", key component of the second liquid, free-water options) could not be opened; how often RadFrac tests is not verified here.
- No open measurement found of the share of column CPU time spent in tangent-plane tests, nor of speed-ups from skipping them inside column solvers (only the reservoir-simulation analogue, seen second-hand).
- Bausa and Marquardt: no paper by Bausa on this topic was found in the searches. Kovach and Seider 1987, Schuil and Bool 1985, Michelsen 1982 were not opened; described only via the ChemSep Book.

## 3. Thermodynamic cost: activity coefficients and enthalpies, analytic vs numerical derivatives, Jacobian reuse, caching

### Takeaway
The derivatives of K (and of excess enthalpy) with respect to T and composition are the expensive part of a Newton
column iteration. Analytic derivatives are reported as "well worth while"; dropping composition derivatives raises
iteration counts or causes failure; hybrid quasi-Newton (exact cheap derivatives, Broyden-updated activity-coefficient
derivatives) saved about 50 % of computer time versus numerical differentiation but not versus analytic derivatives.
Pure quasi-Newton is judged "not especially useful" for separations.

### Cited Findings
- Jacobian block Bj contains dK/dT and dK/dx; "it is rather a painful experience to differentiate, for example, the UNIQUAC equations ... in many SC codes this differentiation is done numerically. This can be an extremely time consuming step." Neglecting these derivatives "is not recommended unless one is dealing with nearly ideal solutions, since, to do so, will almost certainly lead to an increase in the required number of iterations or even to failure" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.4, p. 266-267](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- "Michelson (1986) and Baden and Michelson (1987) have shown that coding analytical derivatives of the complicated physical property models is well worth while as this leads to substantial reductions in the computer time required for this portion of the calculations" (no factor quoted in the Book); ChemSep computes almost all derivatives analytically, except the T-derivative of excess enthalpy (second T-derivative of the activity model) and non-standard specification equations, done by finite differences — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.4, p. 267](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Hybrid Newton/quasi-Newton (Lucia and co-workers): J = C + A, C = cheap derivatives computed exactly, A = activity/fugacity-coefficient composition derivatives updated by a quasi-Newton formula; "while taking more iterations than Newton's method ... generally takes about 50% less computer time to converge (when compared to numerical differentiation but not when compared to analytical differentiation) and has nearly the same region of convergence as Newton's method." "It is probably fair to say that pure quasi-Newton methods are not especially useful for separation process problems" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.4, p. 267](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Selectively retaining property derivatives in J was "counterproductive" for dynamic simulation (Kooijman 1995), so ChemSep does not support it — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.4, p. 268](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Tomich (1970): a 2N Newton-Raphson method using Broyden's update "after just one iteration with the full Newton method" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.3.5, p. 258](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf); DWSIM ships a `Tomich` class in its column solvers — [DWSIM Tomich.vb](https://github.com/DanWBR/dwsim/blob/windows/DWSIM.UnitOperations/UnitOperations/RigorousColumnSolvers/Tomich.vb)
- Structure exploitation: block-tridiagonal Thomas algorithm; A and C blocks nearly empty (Baden 1984; Baden and Michelsen 1987) — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.5, p. 268](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- DWSIM's NS solver (open code) is an example of the slow path: a dense, global, central-difference Jacobian with relative step 0.001 (2n residual evaluations per Jacobian, every one recomputing all stages' K and H), with Broyden updates tried first to avoid rebuilding it — [DWSIM NewtonRaphson.vb, lines 669-705 and 1080-1125](https://github.com/DanWBR/dwsim/blob/windows/DWSIM.UnitOperations/UnitOperations/RigorousColumnSolvers/NewtonRaphson.vb)

### Inferences
- In a stage-grouped Naphtali-Sandholm Jacobian, stage j's K and H depend only on (T_j, x_j, y_j); a per-stage finite difference therefore needs only (2c+1) local perturbations per stage (or c+1 if only T and x enter an activity model with an ideal vapour), each costing one stage's property call, instead of perturbing the whole column. With activity models whose composition derivatives are cheap analytically (NRTL, UNIQUAC), the analytic route removes the finite-difference cost entirely.
- Chord Newton (reuse a factorised Jacobian for several iterations) and Broyden updates trade fewer Jacobian builds for more iterations; the ChemSep Book's evidence (hybrid ≈50 % saving vs numerical, none vs analytic) suggests reuse pays mainly when derivatives are numerical.
- Memoisation candidates follow from the structure: temperature-only terms (vapour pressures, τ_ij(T) of NRTL/UNIQUAC, ideal-gas enthalpies) can be cached per stage per T and reused across the composition perturbations of the same stage.

### Gaps
- No open source found that quantifies the share of column CPU time in activity coefficients vs enthalpies, or the factor gained by analytic derivatives (Baden and Michelsen 1987 not opened).
- No open quantitative results on property caching/memoisation inside column solvers.

## 4. Initialisation: what good starting profiles do for Newton, and warm starts

### Takeaway
Newton needs N(2c+3) initial values; the standard automatic recipe is constant-molar-overflow flows, a T profile from an
ideal-K bubble point, then a few bubble-point (Wang-Henke) sweeps first with ideal and then with the real K model.
Reusing a previous converged column ("old results") is the main practical speed-up in flowsheets, with the caveat
that it can lead to a wrong solution. No quantitative saving was found.

### Cited Findings
- Recipes from the literature: one or more plate-to-plate iterations (Burton and Morton 1987), a tridiagonal method (Wang-Henke for distillation, sum rates for absorption; Wayburn and Seader 1983), short-cut methods (Venkat and Lucia 1987), relaxation (Ketchum 1979), all streams at the combined feed composition (Furzer 1986), or a flash of the combined feeds at average column pressure — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.7, p. 269-270](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- ChemSep's automatic initialisation: constant molar flows; if bottoms flow and reflux ratio cannot be estimated, bottoms = half the feed and reflux ratio = 2 ("this, of course, could cause serious convergence problems", hence optional guesses); compositions and temperatures from "a few iterations of the Bubble Point method": the first with ideal-solution K at the average pressure and an estimate of the combined feed's boiling point (no end temperatures needed), then "Two more iterations of the BP method ... using the (possibly nonideal) K-value model actually selected"; different rules for columns without condenser/reboiler — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.7, p. 270](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- User initialisation: T and/or flow profiles with only first and last stage values required (linear interpolation of T, constant molar flow), compositions from the procedure above — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.10, p. 272](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Initialisation with old results: converged results of a previous run as the guess, interpolated if the number of stages changes; care needed when feed/product specifications or locations change — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.4.11, p. 272](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- In COCO, re-solving a column gives "a significant speed-up" because "COCO remembers the old state of the column and switches the column simulator automatically to a mode where it re-uses the old results as initialization", but "sometimes these old results can lead to the convergence to a wrong solution", so a button deletes them; in flowsheet iterations "old results are used as initialization. This speeds up the convergence when the flowsheet close to being solved (but can sometimes result in a column not being solved)" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 3.1-3.2, p. 93-94](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- For a Petlyuk pair, ChemSep needed user-supplied initialisation for the second column; afterwards "convergence ... can be obtained more easily by using the old results as initialization"; for the n-butanol-water decanter flowsheet, the columns are best solved first with a condenser to estimate the recycle flows — [ChemSep Book, sec. 3.2.2 and 3.2.4, p. 95-96](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- For three-phase columns, Ottenbacher and Hasse initialised all stages at "the same arbitrary starting point inside the miscibility gap" and relied on homotopy to reach the solution — [Ottenbacher and Hasse 2006, p. 844](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/prost/proceedings/distillation06/CD-proceedings/paper082.pdf)

### Inferences
- A warm start from a previous converged column (same components; stage-interpolated if needed) replaces both the cold start and the initial bubble-point sweeps; for a flowsheet with recycles, keeping each column's last converged state between flowsheet iterations is the documented practice of ChemSep/COCO.
- For a three-phase column, a warm start also carries the phase-split pattern (which stages had two liquids), which is exactly the information needed to avoid re-testing every stage (see section 2).

### Gaps
- No open source quantifies the iteration or time saving of ideal-K vs CMO vs warm starts.

## 5. Continuation / homotopy for hard columns, and its cost

### Takeaway
Continuation makes hard (non-ideal, heterogeneous, interlinked) columns converge but costs many more Newton solves;
physically based homotopies (thermodynamic: ideal → real K; efficiency: E_MV small → 1; feed homotopy) are reported
as needing fewer steps than the mathematical Newton homotopy. The recommendation of ChemSep's authors is to try
Newton first and fall back to continuation.

### Cited Findings
- Three categories: mathematical (Newton homotopy: Wayburn and Seader 1983; Bhargava and Hlavacek 1984), physical (thermodynamic homotopies of Vickery and Taylor 1986; Frantz and Van Brunt 1986; parametric continuation in reflux or bottoms flow, Jelinek et al. 1973), proprietary (Byrne and Baird) — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.5, p. 272-273](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Salgovic et al. (1981): integrating the homotopy ODEs accurately was reliable but "the number of function evaluations became quite large"; switching to Newton after a prescribed number of Euler steps was "quite effective" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.5, p. 273](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Bhargava and Hlavacek (1984): one Newton correction per intermediate problem, no predictor, step length 0.2-0.5 sufficed (on problems Newton alone could also solve) — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.5, p. 274](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Ellis et al. (1986): Euler needed the fewest steps, Gear's method the shortest time; semi-implicit Runge-Kutta "disappointing" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.5, p. 274-275](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Kovach and Seider (1986) used the Newton homotopy for heterogeneous azeotropic columns, with separate homotopies for the LLE: start with the two liquids as almost pure binaries and add components along the homotopy, enforcing isoactivity only for the component being added — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.5 and 13.5.3, p. 274, 277](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Thermodynamic homotopy (Vickery and Taylor 1986): "Fewer steps and fewer iterations were needed by the thermodynamic homotopy and the Newton homotopy occasionally failed" on strongly non-ideal systems — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.5.3, p. 277](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Efficiency continuation (Vickery et al. 1988; earlier Muller 1979, Sereno 1985): start at small E_MV, where stages barely separate and K, activity coefficients and excess enthalpies hardly vary from tray to tray, then step E_MV to 1 with Euler predictor and Newton corrector; automatic initialisation; "very effective" for standard specifications but not for product-purity specifications without modification. Woodman (1989, 1990) combined efficiency, thermodynamic and parametric continuation — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.5.5-13.5.6, Table 13.5, p. 277-279](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- "Newton's method should usually be tried before resorting to a continuation method (at least until a continuation method is designed that can outperform Newton's method on problems where both work)" — [ChemSep Book, sec. 13.5.7, p. 279](http://www.chemsep.org/book/docs/book2.pdf)
- Three-phase example: a feed homotopy (only the stage feeds transformed, H = t f + (1−t) g, so only mass and energy balances change) was "even more robust than the widespread Newton homotopy" on lab-scale three-phase distillation with three recycles, where Aspen Plus and direct Newton/Levenberg-Marquardt failed — [Ottenbacher and Hasse 2006, p. 843-844](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/prost/proceedings/distillation06/CD-proceedings/paper082.pdf)
- Review: homotopy-continuation algorithms for steady-state simulation of heterogeneous azeotropic towers and phase-stability analysis in connection with bifurcation points at phase transitions are reviewed in Widagdo and Seider (AIChE J. 42(1), 1996, DOI 10.1002/aic.690420110) (abstract only) — [OSTI record 201433](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/201433)

### Inferences
- Continuation is a robustness tool, not a speed tool: each step is at least one Newton solve with a full Jacobian. As a fallback after a failed (or diverging) plain Newton from a warm or ideal-K start it adds cost only to hard cases; the cheap physical homotopies (ideal-K → activity model; E_MV) reuse the same Newton code.

### Gaps
- No open source found reporting CPU time or step counts of homotopy vs plain Newton on a common benchmark; Vickery and Taylor 1986 and Kovach and Seider 1986/1987 were not opened.
